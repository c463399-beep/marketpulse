Asset categories

The main building blocks of most portfolios, in plain language.

📈

Stocks

Shares of ownership in companies. Prices move with earnings expectations, interest rates, and investor sentiment. Higher long-term growth potential, with more day-to-day volatility than bonds.

🏦

Bonds

Loans you make to governments or corporations. You typically receive interest and your principal back at maturity. Often used to dampen portfolio swings and provide predictable income.

💱

Forex

The foreign-exchange market, where currencies trade against each other (e.g. USD/JPY). Driven by interest-rate differentials, trade flows, and macroeconomic news. Highly liquid and traded around the clock.

🛢️

Commodities

Physical goods such as oil, gold, copper, and agricultural products. Prices respond to supply, demand, geopolitics, and the dollar. Often used for diversification or inflation hedging.

Learn

Short explainers for concepts you will hear often in finance.

Inflation

A sustained rise in the general price level — your money buys less over time. Central banks watch inflation closely and may raise interest rates to cool demand when prices climb too fast.

Interest rates

The cost of borrowing and the reward for saving. When policy rates rise, loans become more expensive and bond prices often fall; when rates fall, the opposite tends to occur. Rates influence almost every asset class.

Diversification

Spreading investments across assets, sectors, and regions so that one bad outcome does not dominate results. It does not eliminate risk, but it can smooth the path toward long-term goals.

P/E ratio

Price-to-earnings: share price divided by earnings per share. A higher P/E can mean investors expect faster growth — or that the stock is expensive relative to current profits. Context and peer comparison matter.

Bull & bear markets

A bull market is a prolonged rise in prices; a bear market is a prolonged decline (often cited as a drop of 20% or more from a recent high). Sentiment, earnings, and liquidity all play a role in each phase.

Risk & return

In general, assets that offer higher expected returns come with greater uncertainty and larger drawdowns. Matching risk to your time horizon and need for stability is a core principle of portfolio design.

Glossary

Quick definitions. Filter by keyword to find a term fast.

ETF
Exchange-traded fund — a basket of securities that trades on an exchange like a stock, often tracking an index.
Yield
Income return on an investment, such as dividends or bond interest, usually expressed as an annual percentage.
Liquidity
How quickly an asset can be bought or sold at a fair price without large price impact. Cash is highly liquid; real estate less so.
Volatility
How much prices bounce around. Higher volatility means larger swings — and often higher perceived risk.
Dividend
A portion of company profits paid to shareholders, usually in cash on a regular schedule.
Market capitalization
Share price × shares outstanding — a common measure of a company’s size in the stock market.
Duration
A measure of how sensitive a bond’s price is to interest-rate changes. Longer duration generally means more rate risk.
Blue chip
Informal term for large, established companies with a long record of stable earnings and often dividends.
Bear market
A prolonged decline in market prices, commonly defined as a drop of 20% or more from a recent peak.
Bull market
A prolonged period of rising prices and generally optimistic investor sentiment.
CPI
Consumer Price Index — a widely used measure of inflation based on a basket of goods and services.
Federal Reserve (Fed)
The central bank of the United States. It sets monetary policy, including the federal funds rate target.
